## Macro: rates, inflation, tariffs, fiscal, AI capex, bubble risk

### Takeaway
By October 2026 the US is in a stagflation-tinged, hawkish regime, not the easing cycle many expected in 2025. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh (in the role since about June 2026) reportedly raised rates to 3.75–4.00% on Sept 16, 2026. Core CPI is about 3.1%. An Iran-war oil shock lifted energy prices. The Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs, which widened the FY2026 deficit to about $2.1T. AI capex is about 2.4% of US GDP, and the bubble debate is mainstream.

### Inferences
- Higher-for-longer, with rates rising, raises the discount rate on long-duration venture assets. It also raises the cost of venture debt, which hit $64.7B in H1 2026. That pressures capital-intensive startups unless they have strategic or sovereign backers.
- The oil shock combined with tariff churn (IEEPA, then 122, then 301) creates real demand for energy efficiency, supply-chain re-routing, customs and trade-compliance software, and domestic manufacturing. These sit outside the AI-crowded zone.
- AI capex is now macro-relevant (about 2.4% of US GDP). A capex pause would hit AI infrastructure startups first. Apps that consume cheaper compute could benefit from overbuild, as apps did after the 2001 telecom overbuild.

NATO's 5% target is driving a multi-year European defense spending ramp. Gulf states are funding sovereign compute at gigawatt scale.

### Inferences
- Sovereign AI demand (Gulf, Europe through Mistral and Nscale, China's domestic stack) means "AI for a specific nation-state" is a real buyer category. The winners there are capital-heavy and politically brokered, which makes them hard for a seed fund to access.
- Gulf physical-security risk (missile and drone strikes on infrastructure) is a new discount on Gulf data center theses. It is also a demand driver for counter-drone, infrastructure hardening and resilient grid and water technology.
- Defense tech is now well capitalized at the top (Anduril, and Founders Fund's war chest). Seed-level whitespace is more likely in the European supply chain: munitions, components, industrial capacity and dual-use manufacturing.

## Demographics and climate adaptation

### Takeaway
US fertility hit another record low in 2025, with a TFR of about 1.57. Natural population growth is projected to end within about a decade, which makes growth immigration-dependent at a time when immigration is slowing. Climate-driven insurance retreat in California and Florida is structural.

### Cited Findings
- 2025 US births were about 3.6M, a general fertility rate of about 53 per 1,000 women. That is down about 1% from 2024 and nearly 20% below two decades earlier. Births rose only among women 30 and older (CDC provisional, Apr 8 2026) — [CNN via KEYT](https://keyt.com/health/cnn-health/2026/04/08/us-fertility-rate-dropped-to-another-record-low-in-2025/)
- 2025 TFR was about 1.57 per woman (WSJ calculation from CDC data) — [WSJ via To Vima](https://www.tovima.com/wsj/why-the-u-s-fertility-rate-has-hit-a-record-low/amp/)
- Census and CBO: the births-over-deaths surplus ends within the next decade, so growth will depend on immigration. Employers face labor headaches as boomers retire and immigration slows (Apr 30 2026) — [HR Brew](https://www.hr-brew.com/stories/2026/04/30/employers-should-expect-future-labor-headaches-as-us-birth-rate-falls-again)
- California: insured losses from the January 2025 LA fires were about $40B. FAIR Plan enrollment rose 43% from Sept 2024 to Dec 2025. State Farm got a 17% emergency rate hike in 2025 and agreed in early 2026 to halt mass non-renewals through 2026. Home insurance costs are up 16.1% since 2023 (single aggregator source; verify with the CA Department of Insurance) — [CoverageCat](https://www.coveragecat.com/insurance-types/home/californias-home-insurance-crisis-whats-happening)
- Florida: average premium above $4,000 against a US average of $1,544 (2023 data; dated) — [Fortune, 2023](https://dc.fortune.com/2023/08/18/housing-market-home-insurance-shock-spells-trouble-for-florida-and-california)

### Inferences
- Labor scarcity in care, trades and logistics combined with an immigration slowdown is the clearest non-consensus pull for AI-plus-robotics "labor equivalents" in physical-world services. Robotics already saw more than $10B in Q3 2026 alone, so humanoids are crowded. Vertical, unglamorous workflow automation (home care scheduling, trades back office, inspection) is less crowded.
- The insurance retreat creates demand for risk modeling, mitigation verification (home hardening), parametric products and alternative-capacity MGAs. These are underwriting businesses where seed checks can work.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable 2026 data on immigration flows, caregiver shortage counts (for example BLS home health aide projections), trades labor shortages, heat or wildfire statistics, or UN global fertility figures.

### Inferences
- **Crowding map.** Crowded: foundation models and labs, AI coding (Cursor at $60B, Cognition), AI inference and infrastructure clouds (Baseten, Crusoe, Nscale), humanoid robotics, and defense primes. Mega-"seed" rounds of $100M+ (about 20% of seed dollars) mean pedigreed AI-researcher seeds price at Series B levels. Underfunded in relative terms: the sub-$100M deal market (12.5% of dollars), non-AI categories, and sub-scale vertical software. Insurance and climate adaptation, care economy, trades and industrial workflow, and the defense supply chain were not mentioned in the mega-round data. Absence from that list suggests relatively lower capital, but I have no quantified evidence of underfunding.
- **20x math at seed.** At a median post-money of about $24M, a 20x return needs roughly a $500M+ exit after dilution. With typical seed-to-exit dilution of 50–60%, the exit needs to be about $1–1.2B+. M&A at $7–13B (OpenRouter, World Labs, Hugging Face) shows that strategic AI M&A can deliver those outcomes for picks-and-shovels layers.
- **Capital concentration at the top** (three firms took 48% of LP dollars) pushes those firms toward $100M+ checks. This leaves less competition at true pre-seed and seed in non-obvious categories, which is the classic edge zone for a smaller fund.
- **Exits favor infrastructure.** Public markets reward AI infrastructure and hard tech (CoreWeave, Circle, CXMT) and punish legacy-style consumer and fintech (Klarna) and generic SaaS. Seed theses should have a credible strategic acquirer or an infrastructure-like profile.

## Contrarian and second-order theses, and historical analogs

### Takeaway
The respected-investor view in 2026 is that frontier models are an oligopoly and becoming infrastructure. Lasting value moves up the stack into apps, workflows, proprietary data and distribution, and AI sells "labor equivalents" rather than software seats. Gil argues consensus is currently right and founders should consider exiting within 12–18 months. Evans argues first movers rarely capture platform-shift value.

### Inferences
- **Second-order synthesis for 20x seed returns**, drawn from the cited views and data:
  1. Apps and workflows on commoditizing models, sold as labor replacement, in verticals with labor scarcity (care, trades, insurance claims, compliance). This aligns Evans, Gil and the demographic data.
  2. Picks and shovels that strategic acquirers will pay for: routers, evals, data and context layers. Stripe/OpenRouter at $7.5B and Nvidia/Hugging Face at $12.9B match Gil's "acquirer competition" point.
  3. Physical-world resilience: energy efficiency and on-site power after the oil shock, insurance and adaptation, the European defense supply chain, and trade compliance after tariff churn.
  4. Overbuild beneficiaries. If capex outpaces revenue (Futuriom, Seeking Alpha), cheap compute later favors consumers of compute over owners. This is the telecom-2001 and cloud analog Evans invokes.
- **Contrarian risk.** Gil's "consensus is right" view implies that fighting the frontier-lab oligopoly at seed is negative expected value. The contrarian edge is in category selection and valuation discipline, not in betting against AI.
- **Historical analog,** paraphrasing Evans's cited claim: in PCs, browsers, search, social and smartphones, the value-capturing companies were often second-wave entrants that built on a mature platform. That suggests 2026–2028 seed vintages could beat 2023–2025 AI vintages if platform costs fall.
